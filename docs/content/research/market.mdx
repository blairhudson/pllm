## Compute and data access are bundled

Remote inference usually asks a provider to do two jobs: supply compute and receive
the client's readable workload. That coupling limits both sides of
the market. Clients can use only operators they trust with sensitive language,
while capable infrastructure cannot serve that demand unless it can satisfy the
same legal, organizational, and jurisdictional requirements as an operator trusted
with client data.

Compute is globally distributed. Trust is not. A more open market needs a way to
buy useful model execution without automatically disclosing prompts, context,
intermediate state, or output to the compute supplier.

## Privacy changes eligible supply

Private inference makes data access a protocol property rather than a prerequisite
for supplying compute. Depending on the design, providers operate on ciphertext,
secret shares, masked values, or data isolated by trusted hardware. The approaches
have different trust assumptions and costs, but pursue the same market boundary:
the supplier performs contracted work without seeing plaintext.

That boundary can make more infrastructure eligible to compete. Regional clouds,
sovereign systems, colocation operators, specialist accelerators, and independent
capacity could serve protected workloads that clients would not send in readable
form. The eligible supply of compute can become broader than the pool of providers
trusted with client data.

## What the market exchanges

The unit of exchange is a private-inference service with an explicit contract, not
an undifferentiated token. Buyers and suppliers need comparable terms:

| Contract dimension | What must be explicit |
| --- | --- |
| Work | Model, precision, context limits, and supported operations |
| Privacy | Protected data, observable metadata, trust assumptions, and failure boundary |
| Performance | Latency, throughput, availability, and capacity |
| Locality | Execution region, jurisdiction, and network path |
| Cost | Compute, bandwidth, preparation, coordination, and settlement |
| Accountability | Authentication, metering, audit evidence, and remedies |

Privacy does not replace discovery, pricing, reliability, or accountability. It
removes plaintext access as the default admission requirement for participating in
those mechanisms.

## Economic mechanism

Removing the need to access client data can widen supplier choice and make substitutable
capacity easier to compare. Buyers gain more options across location, hardware,
availability, performance, and price. Suppliers can compete on useful execution
rather than on their ability to receive the underlying language.

Those effects are possible, not automatic. Privacy overhead can increase compute
and traffic costs, fragmented protocols can prevent portability, and weak service
definitions can hide leakage or shift cost elsewhere. Meaningful competition
depends on full-lifecycle prices and measurements rather than headline token rates.

## Infrastructure still required

A functioning global market needs more than a privacy primitive:

- interoperable workload and privacy contracts;
- provider identity, authorization, and reputation;
- comparable performance and full-lifecycle cost measurements;
- routing across compatible models, hardware, regions, and trust domains;
- privacy-preserving metering, accounting, and settlement;
- failure handling and evidence sufficient to resolve disputes.

Private inference is therefore market-enabling infrastructure, not the market
itself. It creates the separation on which broader discovery, routing, competition,
and settlement can be built.
